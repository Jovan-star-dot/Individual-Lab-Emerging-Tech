# Diffusion Assessment Framework

## Course basis

Primary course source: Andres Fortino, *Mastering the Craft of Emerging Technology Adoption*, Chapter 6, “The Diffusion of Emerging Technologies,” pp. 77–90 (assigned course text). Apply the concepts as the text presents them; the evidence rules below operationalize the assignment and are not additional instructor-defined Rogers thresholds.

The chapter treats diffusion as a social process. Mass communication can create awareness, while trusted interpersonal communication, opinion leaders, and change agents can influence persuasion. Rogers' individual decision stages are knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, and confirmation; confirmation includes continued use or abandonment. Cumulative adoption can form an S-curve and new adopters a bell curve. A Bass fit may be one signal only when adoption series and the addressable population are defensible; sparse early data or the wrong denominator makes projections unreliable.

The course text describes adopter groups approximately as innovators (about 2.5%), early adopters (next 13.5%), early majority (about one-third), late majority (about one-third), and laggards (about 16%). These are approximate conceptual population shares, not universal thresholds for labeling a particular organization or an industry. The five perceived innovation characteristics are relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability.

## Required levels of analysis

| Level | Question | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Technology | How far has Agentic AI generally diffused? | Direct evidence about Agentic AI, not AI or generative AI in general. |
| Application | How far has bounded customer-service resolution using Agentic AI diffused? | Evidence about systems completing customer-service actions, not only answering questions or assisting human agents. |
| Industry/peer group | How far has the application diffused among comparable e-commerce retailers? | Use the named population and geography. If representative peer data are absent, say so and use named examples only as case evidence. |
| Organization | What timing follows for this organization's posture and capabilities? | Use the case facts. Do not alter the general diffusion evidence because this organization is more or less ready. |

## Required classification and characteristic audit

In every response, explicitly assess all five Rogers adopter categories at both the general-technology level and the specific application/peer level. For innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards, say whether the evidence supports a category, supports only named examples, or is insufficient to classify it. “Not classifiable” means the data do not identify that group; it does not mean the group is absent. Use the frozen schema's `general_et_finding` for the technology-level audit and `application_finding` for the application/peer audit.

Also explicitly assess each Rogers characteristic at both levels: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. Use an observed source claim where available and mark each unsupported assessment unknown. Never transfer general AI survey findings to Agentic AI or transfer one application deployment to the overall technology category. In the application field, make industry/comparable-peer diffusion a distinct statement from evidence about the individual application.

Within the same allowed fields, identify evidenced change agents, opinion leaders, ecosystem and complementary assets, standards, and inhibitors; state when the sources do not identify them. Do not make claims about peer influence or standards adoption from an announcement alone.

## Evidence ladder and adopter categories

For each source, record publication date, measurement period, population, geography, technology, application, denominator, and whether it measures awareness, plans, pilots, production deployment, repeated use, or continuation/abandonment. Treat evidence strength roughly in this order: representative longitudinal measures of the exact application; representative current measures of the exact application; multiple independently documented production and continued-use cases; single documented deployment or pilot; survey intentions, planned investment, vendor claims, media coverage, or generalized predictions.

Map evidence to Rogers' categories only when the relevant population and adoption denominator are known. A small set of early deployments can show that innovators or early adopters exist; it cannot establish what share of the population is in a category. Do not call an application “mainstream” from attention, plans, a broad AI-use rate, or several large-company announcements. Do not fit an S-curve or Bass model without time-series observations and a defensible addressable population.

## Rogers' five characteristics

Assess for both Agentic AI generally and the named application when evidence supports it. Identify whose perceptions matter (buyers, service staff, customers, technology teams) and distinguish their views.

| Characteristic | Assessment prompt |
|---|---|
| Relative advantage | What measurable improvement over existing service processes is observed, by whom, and against what baseline? Separate claimed from independently measured benefits. |
| Compatibility | Does it fit existing systems, workflows, policies, data access, customer expectations, and organizational norms? Identify integration or policy gaps. |
| Complexity | What skills, controls, maintenance, vendor support, and process changes are required? Do not infer simplicity from a polished demo. |
| Trialability | Can an organization or user test it with bounded exposure and reversible commitment? Distinguish a pilot from broad adoption. |
| Observability | Can peers see credible outcomes and continued use? A press release or demo is visibility, not proof of realized benefit. |

## Social and ecosystem forces

Identify named change agents (people or teams deliberately promoting adoption) separately from opinion leaders (trusted peers whose experience influences others). Consider vendors, integrators, industry bodies, standards/protocols, complementary assets such as usable APIs and clean data, cloud/customer-service platforms, workforce skills, and legal, security, trust, cost, or reliability inhibitors. Record whether each factor is directly observed, source-reported, or inferred. A standard's existence or one firm's adoption does not show that the standard is widely implemented.

## Organization and timing

Characterize the organization as more like an innovator/early adopter, fast follower, or conservative adopter from the case evidence. For a same-technology/same-application contrast, carry forward the same general and application diffusion findings. Then explain how posture, compatibility, integration capacity, and evidence tolerance change the timing implication. State whether the implication is to act sooner, monitor, or wait for stronger peer evidence; do not make the complete adoption decision or plan a rollout.

## Current evidence packet for this assignment

The following claims are source-reported. Their limits must travel with them into the assessment. They do not establish a representative Agentic AI adoption rate.

1. **U.S. retail AI-use context — U.S. Census Bureau, May 26, 2026.** The Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) reports that, as of the collection period ending May 3, 2026, around 14% of businesses in the Retail Trade sector used AI; around 17% expected use in the next six months. BTOS is a biweekly, nationally representative survey of U.S. businesses, but the relevant question concerns AI used in any business function in the prior two weeks / expected in the next six months. It does not isolate Agentic AI, e-commerce, customer-service resolution, autonomous actions, depth of use, or continued use. It is broad AI context only. Source: https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html
2. **Retailer-level priorities and inhibitors — National Retail Federation Center for Digital Risk & Innovation, report dated December 17, 2025; survey fielded summer 2025.** The report describes 56 AI leaders at U.S.-based NRF member retailers contacted through NRF councils/working groups. The report says 57% cited AI service/tool cost and 57% model accuracy/reliability as top strategic challenges; 55% cited workforce AI-expertise gaps. It also says 75% had implemented or were implementing AI for IT coding/development, while customer service resolution was not measured as a standalone Agentic AI application. This small, selected, self-reported respondent group is not a representative estimate of all retailers or Agentic AI use. It supplies evidence of perceived inhibitors, not an application adoption rate. Source: https://nrf.com/research/retail-ai-trends-2025
3. **Walmart customer support — Walmart corporate technology account dated May 29, 2025.** Walmart says agents in its Customer Support Assistant were routing and resolving issues and increasingly acting autonomously; it also describes a multi-agent shopping assistant. The source is a company statement about Walmart's systems and customers; it gives no denominator, geography for the service deployment, adoption rate, independent evaluation, or sustained-use rate. It is a named large-retailer deployment signal, not market prevalence. Source: https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2025/05/29/inside-walmarts-strategy-for-building-an-agentic-future
4. **Crocs customer-facing service agent — Salesforce customer story dated September 10, 2026.** Salesforce reports that Crocs built a multi-agent shopping/service assistant, with the first release covering discovery, FAQs, order tracking, returns, and checkout; it reports three agents built in five months and a voice Help Agent built/launched in seven weeks. Salesforce describes a vendor-supported customer-ready implementation, not an independently audited adoption measure. Exact retail deployment geography, number of active users, transaction volume, sustained use, and independently measured outcomes are not reported. The source reports use of existing order-management APIs and partner/FDE expertise, evidence of complementary assets in that single deployment. It also says Crocs adopted MCP; this is one reported implementation, not evidence of retail-wide standards uptake. Source: https://www.salesforce.com/customer-stories/crocs/building-ai-agents/

**Strongest diffusion signal available:** two named large retailers publicly describe Agentic AI systems doing customer-facing service work, including routing/resolving support and a Crocs service agent spanning order tracking and returns. These examples suggest early production activity among selected, well-resourced retailers. They do not establish the population share, peer-category distribution, or sustained adoption rate.

**Most important inhibitor supported by available evidence:** accuracy/reliability concerns for customer-facing AI, with cost and workforce expertise close behind in NRF's broad-AI survey. For action-taking service agents, the consequences of incorrect order or return actions make reliability especially material. This application-specific weighting is an inference, not an Agentic AI survey result. Compatibility/integration is also a plausible inhibitor: Crocs' account highlights existing APIs, specialist partners, and a trusted data foundation, but a single successful case cannot quantify how common that constraint is.

**Required uncertainty:** current public evidence is too narrow and heterogeneous to confidently assign Agentic AI for e-commerce customer-service resolution to a Rogers' majority stage or to estimate diffusion percentages. Describe observed early deployments, not a representative stage. Say what evidence would improve confidence: dated, representative, geography-specific retailer data that distinguishes agentic/autonomous actions, customer-service resolution, pilots versus production, adoption denominators, and continued use or abandonment.
